## 8:02 · Mejora recursiva

**[8:02]** What is recursive self-improvement?

**[8:04]** Recursive self-improvement is the idea of an AI an AI system being able to contribute to its own development and then that AI system be able to being able to contribute to the development of the next version and so on and so on. Are there problems to recursive self-improvement?

**[8:22]** Are there benefits to recursive self-improvement?

**[8:24]** Why does it matter?

**[8:26]** One of the issues with recursive self-improvement is that it increasingly means that humans are taken out of the loop of the development. So at the moment or at least until very recently, humans were heavily involved in every stage of researching and developing a new AI system. But increasingly the AI is writing code, designing experiments, taking part in the whole process. and fewer and fewer humans have insight into actually what's going on and how that process works.

**[8:55]** As that continues, we're going to have less insight into what's going on with these systems, even less than we already have. And the speed of development is going to get faster and faster. At the moment, the rate is somewhat bottlenecked on human capacity. There's only so many hours a human can work. There's only so only so many people in the world who can be working on this. But once we have AI systems that are capable of doing this work, we can copy them, we can scale them up, we can make them run really fast and the feedback loops for developing the next system and then the next system are going to get shorter and shorter until something that used to take weeks, months, years happens in much much shorter time scales.

**[9:35]** From a perspective of developing AI capabilities, recursive self-improvement is great. It can move a lot faster. But from the perspective of wanting to make sure that humans remain in control of their technology and that it's developed safely, it seems pretty scary.

## 15:32 · Posibles efectos contraproducentes de las evaluaciones

**[15:32]** Can you tell us a little bit or give specific examples of backfire risks that you're concerned about?

**[15:38]** One of the things I worked on at OpenAI was dangerous capability evaluations. The idea here is to try and find ways to test models for potentially dangerous capabilities like knowledge of boweapons or hacking skills. For example, one approach for policy could be to mandate that all frontier labs have to run some kind of set of these dangerous capability evaluations and if the model exceeds the thresholds, then it can't be deployed. I worry that that would lead to a false sense of security.

**[16:13]** There are a bunch of ways in which the evaluations can't be comprehensive enough. There are ways in which the models can get smart enough to hide their capabilities and make it look like they're less capable than they are. And currently we don't really have any good ways of detecting that kind of thing. I do think overall having something like dangerous capability evaluations is better than having literally nothing at all. But I just want people to see this as one piece of the puzzle and there are many other problems, many other approaches that we need to have in place in order to be actually secure here.
